Decision Trees: Playing 20 Questions 🌲

Level: Beginner Topic: Node Splitting, Entropy & Gini Impurity ← Lesson 0005

1. WHY Do We Need Decision Trees? (Drawbacks of Math-Heavy Feature Scaling)

Have you ever played 20 Questions? If you want to guess a secret object, asking "Is it an animal?" is a great first question because it splits all objects into two neat groups. Asking "Is it named Bob?" is terrible because it barely separates anything!

A Decision Tree learns by asking a sequence of Yes/No questions on features to separate data into pure groups.

🌍 Real-World Industry Context & Case Studies

Decision Trees are prized in industry whenever interpretability and compliance are mandatory:

  • 🏦 Financial Credit & Loan Approvals: Banking regulators require institutions to explain exactly why an applicant was denied a loan (e.g. IF Income < $40k AND Credit Score < 650 THEN Deny).
  • 🏥 Emergency Medical Triage: ER flowcharts use decision trees to route patients to ICU based on vital signs (Heart Rate, Oxygen Saturation, Blood Pressure).
  • 📡 Telecom Customer Churn: Customer success teams extract simple decision rules to identify subscribers at risk of canceling.

2. WHAT IS IT? (Playing 20 Questions to Partition Feature Space)

A. Entropy: The Measure of Chaos

Entropy $H(S)$ measures how mixed up or chaotic a dataset is:

  • Pure Node (5 Apples, 0 Oranges): Chaos = 0. Entropy $H = 0$.
  • Maximum Chaos Node (5 Apples, 5 Oranges): Chaos is high! Entropy $H = 1.0$.
Shannon Entropy Formula
\[ H(S) = - \sum_{i=1}^{C} p_i \log_2(p_i) \] where $p_i$ is the fraction of items belonging to class $i$.

B. Gini Impurity (The Faster Alternative)

Calculating logarithms in Entropy takes CPU power. Many trees (like Random Forests) use Gini Impurity instead:

Gini Impurity Formula
\[ G(S) = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{C} p_i^2 \]

3. HOW Does It Work? (Calculating Entropy $H(S)$, Gini Impurity, and Information Gain)

Information Gain is the reduction in Entropy achieved by splitting a node on a feature:

\[ \text{Information Gain} = H(\text{Parent}) - \left[ \frac{N_{\text{left}}}{N} H(\text{Left}) + \frac{N_{\text{right}}}{N} H(\text{Right}) \r\right] \]

The AI tests every possible question and picks the one with the Highest Information Gain!

⚠️ Overfitting Trap & Tree Pruning Hyperparameters

If allowed to grow without limits, a Decision Tree will create a leaf node for every single sample—achieving 100% training accuracy, but failing miserably on new test data!

  • Pre-Pruning Hyperparameters (Early Stopping):
    • max_depth: Limits how deep the tree can grow (e.g. depth 3-6).
    • min_samples_split: Minimum samples required in a node to attempt a new split.
    • min_samples_leaf: Minimum samples required inside a terminal leaf node.
  • Gini Impurity ($G$) vs. Entropy ($H$): \[ G(S) = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{C} p_i^2, \quad H(S) = -\sum_{i=1}^{C} p_i \log_2(p_i) \] Gini is faster to compute (no logarithms) and is the default in Scikit-Learn.

⚡ Practical Decision Guide: Decision Trees vs. Linear Models

Feature Decision Trees Linear / Logistic Models
Feature Scaling Requirement None (Monotonic invariant) Strictly Required (Z-score / Normalization)
Non-Linear Relationships Handles automatically via recursive splits Requires manual polynomial feature engineering
Outlier Sensitivity Low (Splits based on rank order) High (Outliers distort slope / intercept)
Primary Limitation High Variance / Easy Overfitting High Bias / Cannot fit complex non-linear shapes
