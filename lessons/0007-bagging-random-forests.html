Bagging & Random Forests 🌲🌲🌲

Level: Beginner Topic: Bootstrap Aggregating, Feature Subsampling & Variance Reduction ← Lesson 0006

1. WHY Do We Need Random Forests? (Drawbacks of Deep Decision Tree Overfitting)

If you have a rare medical symptom, would you trust the opinion of a single doctor who might have a personal bias, or an ensemble of 100 diverse doctors voting together?

A single Decision Tree often overfits noise in training data (High Variance). Ensemble Learning combines many trees to build a super-model that is far more accurate than any single tree!

🌍 Real-World Industry Context & Case Studies

Random Forests are the gold standard baseline model for tabular business data:

  • 🛍️ E-Commerce Recommendation & Churn: Retailers use Random Forests to rank customer purchase probability because they handle missing user demographics seamlessly.
  • 🏡 Real Estate Valuation: Estimating property values across thousands of heterogeneous neighborhood features.
  • 🏥 Medical Diagnosis Systems: Aggregating predictions across hundreds of trees prevents individual doctor bias and delivers robust clinical risk scores.

2. WHAT IS IT? (Wisdom of Crowds: Bootstrapping & Feature Subsampling)

Bagging works in two simple steps:

Step 1: Bootstrapping (Random Row Sampling)
Create $B$ different training datasets by randomly picking rows from the original data with replacement (some rows repeat, some are left out).
Step 2: Aggregating (Majority Vote or Average)
Train $B$ trees independently in parallel on those datasets.
• For Classification: Take the Majority Vote.
• For Regression: Take the Average Prediction.

3. HOW Does It Work? (Variance Reduction Math $\text{Var} \to \frac{\sigma^2}{B}$ & Majority Voting)

If one feature is super strong (e.g., "Income" for predicting credit risk), every bootstrapped tree will put "Income" at the root split. The trees will all be identical and correlated!

Random Forest Feature Subsampling
At every split in every tree, the Random Forest considers only a random subset of $m$ features (typically $m = \sqrt{M}$ total features).

Variance Reduction Math:

If $B$ trees have variance $\sigma^2$ and correlation $\rho$, the total variance of the ensemble prediction is:

\[ \text{Variance}_{\text{Ensemble}} = \rho \sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B} \sigma^2 \]

By forcing trees to use random feature subsets, we drive correlation $\rho → 0$, causing variance to drop toward $\frac{\sigma^2}{B}$!

🎁 Out-of-Bag (OOB) Error & Feature Importance
  • Out-of-Bag (OOB) Free Validation: When drawing bootstrap samples with replacement, mathematically $\approx 36.8\%$ of data points are left out of each tree:
    • \[ \lim_{N \to \infty} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{N} \right)^N = \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.368 \] These left-out "OOB samples" act as a built-in validation set! You get free model evaluation without holding out test data.
  • Feature Importance (MDI): Random Forests calculate feature importance by tracking how much each feature reduces Gini Impurity across all trees in the forest.
  • Random Feature Subsampling ($m = \sqrt{P}$): If one dominant feature exists, every tree would split on it first! Sampling only $\sqrt{P}$ features per split forces trees to look at different attributes, decorrelating the ensemble.

⚡ Practical Decision Guide: Single Tree vs. Bagging vs. Random Forests

Algorithm Bias / Variance Parallel Training Validation Mechanism When to Use
Single Decision Tree Low Bias / High Variance N/A Requires Train-Test Split When maximum visual rule interpretability is required
Bagged Trees Low Bias / Reduced Variance Yes (Fully Parallel) OOB Score available Ensembling unstable base estimators
Random Forest Low Bias / Lowest Variance Yes (Fully Parallel) OOB Score + Feature Importance Default go-to model for structured tabular data
